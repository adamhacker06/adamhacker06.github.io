Part 1: Masses and springs

The cloth is modeled as a grid of num_width_points × num_height_points point masses connected by springs. In Cloth::buildGrid, I first create all the point masses, evenly spaced so that the first mass is at (0, 0) and the last is at (width, height) in the cloth's plane. The spacing is width / (num_width_points - 1) horizontally and height / (num_height_points - 1) vertically. For a HORIZONTAL cloth, the masses lie in the xz-plane at y = 1. For a VERTICAL cloth, they lie in the xy-plane, and each mass gets a small random z-offset in [-1/1000, 1/1000] from rand(). The masses are stored in row-major order, so the mass at grid position (x, y) is at index y * num_width_points + x. Any mass whose (x, y) index appears in the pinned list is marked as pinned.

After all masses exist (so the vector won't reallocate and invalidate pointers), I loop over the grid again and add three kinds of springs, skipping any whose other end would fall outside the grid:

  • Structural: to the mass directly to the left and the mass directly above. These form the basic square grid and resist stretching.
  • Shearing: to the masses diagonally up-left and up-right. These form an "X" in each grid cell and resist shearing (in-plane skewing).
  • Bending: to the mass two to the left and the mass two above. These skip a mass and resist folding/bending out of plane.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

Each call to Cloth::simulate advances the cloth by one time step delta_t in three stages.

  1. Forces. I reset every point mass's forces to the total external force \( F = m \sum a_{\text{ext}} \) (e.g. gravity). Then, for each spring whose constraint type is enabled, I apply Hooke's law \[ F_s = k_s \left( \lVert p_a - p_b \rVert - l \right) \] along the direction between the two masses, adding it to one end and subtracting it from the other. Bending springs use \( 0.2\,k_s \) so they are weaker than structural and shearing springs.
  2. Verlet integration. For each unpinned mass I compute the new position with damped Verlet integration, \[ x_{t+dt} = x_t + (1 - d)(x_t - x_{t-dt}) + a_t\, dt^2, \] where \( a_t = F / m \) and \( d \) is the damping percentage divided by 100. The old position is saved as last_position.
  3. Constraining position updates (Provot 1995). If a spring ends up more than 10% longer than its rest length, I pull its two masses toward each other along the spring until it is exactly 10% longer. The correction is split evenly between the two masses, or applied entirely to the free one if the other is pinned. If both are pinned, nothing is done. This keeps the cloth from stretching unrealistically in a single step.

Experimenting with parameters

All of these use scene/pinned2.json. The default parameters are \( k_s = 5000 \) N/m, density = 15 g/cm², and damping = 0.2%.

Spring constant \( k_s \). With a very low \( k_s \) (500 N/m), the springs are weak, so the cloth stretches a lot under its own weight. The top edge between the two pinned corners droops into a deep curve, the cloth hangs a little lower overall, and there are visible wrinkles bunched near the top. With a very high \( k_s \) (50000 N/m), the springs resist stretching strongly, so the cloth is stiff. The top edge stays almost straight with only a shallow dip, and the surface is smoother with fewer wrinkles.

Density. Density sets the mass of each point mass. Gravity's pull scales with mass while the spring forces don't, so density has roughly the opposite effect of \( k_s \). A high density (150 g/cm²) makes the cloth heavy relative to its springs, so it stretches and sags more. A low density (1 g/cm²) makes the cloth light, so the springs dominate and it behaves stiffly. In fact, the high-density result looks almost identical to the low-\( k_s \) result, and the low-density result looks almost identical to the high-\( k_s \) result. This makes sense because the acceleration from a spring is \( F_s / m \), so multiplying density by 10 has about the same effect as dividing \( k_s \) by 10.

Damping. Damping removes a fraction of each mass's velocity every step, which simulates energy lost to friction and air resistance. With zero damping, no energy is lost, so the cloth swings wildly and keeps moving instead of settling. Mid-swing, it crumples and folds over itself into chaotic wrinkles. With high damping (1%, the slider maximum), the cloth falls slowly and smoothly, as if through a thick fluid, and swings down to rest with no crumpling or overshoot. Damping mostly changes how the cloth moves on the way to rest, not the final shape.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

After the Verlet step in Cloth::simulate, I loop over every unpinned point mass and call collide on every collision object in the scene. Each collide checks whether the mass ended up inside the object and, if so, moves it back to the surface. Both use friction \( f \) by moving the mass only a fraction \( (1 - f) \) of the way from its last_position to the surface point, which slows down sliding along the surface.

Spheres. A mass is inside the sphere if its distance from the center is at most the radius. In that case I find the tangent point by extending the line from the center through the mass's current position out to the surface: \[ p_{\text{tangent}} = c + r \, \frac{p - c}{\lVert p - c \rVert}. \] The correction vector is \( p_{\text{tangent}} - p_{\text{last}} \), and the new position is \( p_{\text{last}} + (1 - f)\,(p_{\text{tangent}} - p_{\text{last}}) \).

Planes. A mass collides with the plane if it crossed from one side to the other during the last time step. I check this with the signed distances \( d_{\text{last}} = (p_{\text{last}} - q) \cdot n \) and \( d = (p - q) \cdot n \), where \( q \) is a point on the plane and \( n \) is its unit normal. If they have opposite signs (or the mass landed exactly on the plane), it crossed. The tangent point is the current position projected onto the plane, \( p - d\,n \). I then nudge it by SURFACE_OFFSET along the normal toward the side the mass came from, so it stays just above the plane instead of on it. The new position is again \( p_{\text{last}} \) plus \( (1 - f) \) times the correction to that point.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Checking every pair of point masses against each other would be \( O(n^2) \), which is too slow, so I used spatial hashing. I split space into 3D boxes of size \( w \times h \times t \), where \( w = 3 \cdot \text{width} / \text{num\_width\_points} \), \( h = 3 \cdot \text{height} / \text{num\_height\_points} \), and \( t = \max(w, h) \). Only masses in the same box are checked against each other.

  • hash_position: I find which box a position falls in by dividing each coordinate by the box size and taking the floor, which gives integer box indices \( (i, j, k) \). I combine them into one number, \( i + P(j + Pk) \) with \( P = 113 \), and return it as a float. The cloth only spans a few dozen boxes along each axis, so different boxes get different keys.
  • build_spatial_map: at the start of each self-collision pass, I clear the map and add a pointer to every point mass into the list for its box's key.
  • self_collide: for a given mass, I look up the other masses in its box, skipping itself. For each one closer than \( 2 \cdot \text{thickness} \), I compute the correction that would push this mass directly away until they are exactly \( 2 \cdot \text{thickness} \) apart. I average these corrections and divide by simulation_steps before applying it, so each step makes only a small, gradual adjustment instead of a sudden jump.

In Cloth::simulate, after the Verlet step, I rebuild the spatial map and call self_collide on every unpinned point mass. This runs before the sphere/plane collisions and the 10% stretch constraint.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small program that runs on the GPU, in parallel, for every vertex or every pixel being drawn. Because thousands of copies run at once, the GPU can compute lighting for the whole cloth in real time, instead of the minutes per frame our CPU ray tracer took. A program is made of two parts that run one after the other:

  • The vertex shader runs once per vertex. It transforms the vertex's position into world space and screen space (gl_Position), and it can also move the vertex (as in displacement mapping). It passes per-vertex values like world position, normal, tangent, and uv to the next stage.
  • The GPU then rasterizes each triangle into fragments (roughly pixels) and interpolates the vertex shader's outputs across the triangle. The fragment shader runs once per fragment and uses those interpolated values, plus uniforms like the light and camera position and textures, to compute the final color.

Together, the vertex shader decides where the surface is and what its geometry looks like, and the fragment shader decides what color each point of it should be. Lighting and material effects come from the fragment shader combining the surface normal, light direction, view direction, and textures.

Blinn-Phong shading

Blinn-Phong approximates the light reflected by a surface as the sum of three terms: \[ L = k_a I_a + k_d \,(I / r^2)\, \max(0, n \cdot l) + k_s \,(I / r^2)\, \max(0, n \cdot h)^p. \]

  • Ambient (\( k_a I_a \)): a constant amount of light everywhere, standing in for light bounced around the scene, so parts facing away from the light aren't pure black.
  • Diffuse (\( k_d \)): the matte part. It's brightest where the surface faces the light directly (\( n \cdot l \) close to 1), and doesn't depend on where the viewer is.
  • Specular (\( k_s \)): the shiny highlight. \( h \) is the half vector between the light direction and the view direction, so \( n \cdot h \) is large when the surface would reflect the light toward the camera. The exponent \( p \) controls how tight the highlight is.

I used \( k_a = 0.1 \), \( I_a = 1 \), \( k_d \) = the color chosen in the GUI, \( k_s = 0.5 \), and \( p = 100 \). Both the diffuse and specular terms fall off with the squared distance to the light, \( I / r^2 \).

Bump and displacement mapping

Both use the same height map, textures/texture_3.png (a brick pattern), instead of the default texture_2.png. The height \( h(u, v) \) is the red channel of that texture.

Bump mapping only changes the normals, in the fragment shader. I build the TBN matrix from the tangent, bitangent \( b = n \times t \), and normal, and measure how much the height changes over one texel in u and in v: \( dU = (h(u + 1/w, v) - h(u, v))\, k_h k_n \) and \( dV = (h(u, v + 1/h) - h(u, v))\, k_h k_n \). The local normal \( (-dU, -dV, 1) \) is multiplied by TBN to get the new world-space normal, which is then shaded with Blinn-Phong.

Displacement mapping does the same thing in the fragment shader, but the vertex shader also moves each vertex along its normal: \( p' = p + n \, h(u, v)\, k_h \). So the geometry itself changes, not just the lighting.

Bump mapping makes the surface look bumpy because the lighting changes across the bricks, but the outline of the sphere stays perfectly round. Displacement mapping actually pushes the bricks outward, so the silhouette becomes bumpy too and the bricks cast more convincing shading.

Sphere mesh coarseness

Bump mapping looks about the same at both resolutions, because it works per fragment and doesn't depend on how many vertices the sphere has. Displacement mapping depends on the mesh, because it only moves vertices. With the coarse 16×16 sphere, the vertices are far apart, so the bricks can't really change the shape. The only visible effect is a slightly bumpy, faceted outline, while the brick detail itself still comes from the shading. With the 128×128 sphere, the vertices are close enough that the outline follows the height map more smoothly. The silhouette is where displacement differs from bump mapping: bump mapping always keeps a perfectly round outline. With a small height scaling of 0.02 the geometric change is subtle at both resolutions.

Mirror shader

The mirror shader computes the outgoing eye ray \( w_o \) from the surface point toward the camera, reflects it across the normal to get \( w_i = \text{reflect}(-w_o, n) \), and samples the environment cube map in that direction.
