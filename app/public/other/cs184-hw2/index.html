Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

The main idea of the de Casteljau algorithm is to recursively calculate the middle points between control points using a parameter \( t \). The formula for getting these intermediate points is determined by: \[ P_i^{(k)} = (1 - t)\, P_i^{(k-1)} + t\, P_{i+1}^{(k-1)} \] You are essentially doing linear interpolation between the control points, and then repeating this step many times until you reach one point. This is the evaluated point on the Bezier curve. In the implemented code, I created a function that does one step of this interpolation. The engine then repeats this until a single point is evaluated.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

For Bezier surfaces, the de Casteljau algorithm is applied in two dimensions. To start, there is a grid of control points. The process is to first evaluate the surface in one direction (for example, the u-directionn). This is completed by acting like each row of control points is a separate Bezier curve and applying the de Casteljau algorithm to each row. After finishing this and getting the intermediate points, the algorithm is then applied again in the other direction (for example, the v-direction) using these intermediate points. This then gives the final evaluated point on the Bezier surface.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

To get the normal at a vertex, I iterate through the half-edge structure and go to every triangle that touches it. Then, for each triangle, I take the cross product of two of its edges. The direction of this normal faces outward and is twice the length of the triangle's area. I then add this normal to an overall total. After iterating through all the triangles, I normalize the total to get the final vertex normal. This is done for every vertex in the mesh. Phong shading interpolates these normals along the surface of the triangle, which is what makes it smooth. The default flat shading uses the triangle's normal for the entire triangle, which is why it looks flat.

To start loop subdivsion, I made a new position for every orginal vertex using a weighted average of the vertex and its neighbors (n is the vertex's degree): \[ P' = (1 - n u)\, P + u \sum_{i=1}^{n} P_i, \qquad u = \begin{cases} 3/16 & n = 3 \\ 3/(8n) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] Then, I got a new position for each edge's new midpoint vertex by using the two endpoints of the edge and the two opposite vertices. The formula for this is: \[ M = \frac{3}{8}(A + B) + \frac{1}{8}(C + D) \] Then, I split every edge and flipped edges that connected an old vertex to a new vertex, using splitEdge(), flipEdge() and keeping track of component history with the isNew flag. Then, I updated the positions of all vertices to their new positions by copying newPosition to position. As part of this process, I performed subdivison on a cube multiple times and observed some asymmetry. This makes sense because subdivision averages out the positions of vertices and each face of this cube was only defined by one diagonal (two half triangles). This means that the diagonals of each face were not symmetric, which caused the asymmetry in the final result. To try to fix this, I did some preprocessing and split each face's diagonal first.
